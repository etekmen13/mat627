\section{Discussion}
\subsection*{Experiment 1}
For the smooth sine function, the local methods are the most reliable. The piecewise quadratic
interpolant and the cubic spline converge cleanly, with the spline showing  fourth-order
convergence. The Hermite and global Lagrange formulas become extremely accurate at moderate $N$,
but by the largest values they also show the finite-precision instability expected from high-degree
global divided-difference polynomials. Among the global Lagrange methods, the Chebyshev nodes
are more stable than the equally spaced nodes.

\subsection*{Experiment 2}
The cusp at $x=0$ causes problems for all methods. The global Lagrange interpolant with equally spaced
nodes becomes rapidly unstable as $N$ grows, while the piecewise cubic and spline approximations
continue to improve with a much more modest rate. That slower decay is still consistent with the
limited smoothness of $f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x^2}$: the local methods are stable, but the singular
derivative prevents the high-order convergence seen in the smooth experiments.

\subsection*{Experiment 3}
When $b=1$, every method is reasonablyaccurate, but the Chebyshev-node interpolant already 
has the smallest global oscillation. When $b=4$, the equally spaced global Lagrange interpolant
develops the large endpoint oscillations as described by the Runge phenomenon, while the Chebyshev,
piecewise cubic, and spline approximations stay substantially more stable. 

\subsection*{Graduate Section}
The collocation spline produces a smooth approximation on the full interval and the error table
shows second-order decay. Because the approximation is represented
directly as a cubic spline, the boundary value problem is solved over the whole interval rather
than only at the mesh points, so the plotted error is smooth instead of like with a finite difference method.
